MINH LE

Behind

Artist Statement / Bio

What began as a simple hobby has grown into a focused practice centered on how digital systems shape perception and interaction. I work across graphic design, photography, and real time media, using tools such as TouchDesigner, Blender, and Adobe software. I build projects that test how images, data, and user input interact. This interest comes from a drive to understand how technical systems structure what we see, how we move, and how we respond. I engage with new tools because they change the conditions of making and viewing. Real time engines, sensors, and generative systems shift authorship and introduce unpredictability. I draw on ideas from media theory and human-computer interaction to question what these systems are designed to do, who they serve, and how they guide behavior. My work treats technology as both a medium and a subject. I create interactive environments that engage multiple senses through motion, sound, touch, and spatial awareness. These works require participation. Viewers trigger changes, navigate space, and shape outcomes through their presence and input. My practice centers on building and testing. I prototype systems, observe how people engage with them, and refine the work through iteration. This process focuses on clarity, response, and connection between body, space, and system.

Project

Behind

18 / Digital [afterimage]

Artwork Statement

My installation, Behind, explores how social anxiety and depression affect the body. It places the viewer in a confined space where movement, vision, interaction, and control become limited. The work creates feelings of isolation, hesitation, discomfort, and entrapment through a closed door, a restricted view, an awkward posture, and a repetitive digital environment. The installation centers on a single door measuring approximately eight feet tall, three feet wide, and five feet deep. The door remains closed, preventing the viewer from entering. A peephole provides the only view into the space beyond. Instead of a traditional handle, the door contains an open space where the viewer must reach inside. This altered entry point turns a familiar action into an uncertain one. The viewer must interact with something they cannot fully see. A computer mouse serves as the controller inside the opening. The viewer must reach into the door and move the mouse to control the visual environment. Since the mouse remains outside the viewer’s direct line of sight, the interaction requires careful movement and continued attention. The viewer’s ability to control the scene depends on reaching into a restricted space, connecting agency with hesitation and physical limitation. Through the peephole, the viewer sees a hallway looping endlessly. The hallway suggests movement without progress. No matter how long the viewer controls the scene, the visual path leads back to the same place. Voices of doubt repeat throughout the environment, building pressure and reinforcing the feeling of mental exhaustion. The viewer moves forward while remaining trapped within the same cycle. Each element translates an emotional experience into a physical action. The closed door establishes a boundary. The peephole limits vision and positions the viewer as an outsider. The open space replaces the expected function of a doorknob with uncertainty. The mouse offers control, but its placement makes control difficult and incomplete. The looping hallway connects physical repetition with the mental cycles associated with anxiety and depression. I am interested in how the body responds when interaction feels restricted, unsafe, or uncomfortable. My interest comes from observing how anxiety affects young adult men, especially when emotional expression and social interaction feel difficult. Rather than explaining anxiety and depression through a narrative, Behind places the viewer in a situation where hesitation becomes part of the experience. Looking, reaching, moving, and controlling become physical expressions of emotional states.
